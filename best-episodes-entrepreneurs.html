1

Alex Hormozi — How to Build a $100M Business

One of the most replayed Diary of a CEO episodes of all time

Alex Hormozi broke down his entire framework for building businesses that generate over $100 million in revenue. This wasn't motivational fluff — it was a masterclass in offers, pricing, and lead generation that any entrepreneur at any stage can apply. Hormozi's clarity on what actually drives revenue versus what entrepreneurs waste time on is worth more than most MBA programmes.

"Most entrepreneurs are trying to find more customers for their products. Instead, they should be finding more products for their customers." — Alex Hormozi
🎯 Key Takeaway: The fastest path to scaling isn't acquiring new customers — it's creating irresistible offers that increase the lifetime value of the customers you already have. Focus on offer creation before marketing.

2

Sara Blakely — The Spanx Founder on Failure and Resilience

How a $5,000 investment became a billion-dollar brand

Sara Blakely shared the raw, unfiltered story of building Spanx from nothing — selling fax machines door to door while prototyping shapewear in her apartment. Her conversation with Steven Bartlett revealed how she trained herself to embrace failure, why she kept her idea secret for a year, and the unconventional sales tactics that landed her product in Neiman Marcus without any connections.

"My dad used to ask us at dinner, 'What did you fail at this week?' If we had nothing, he'd be disappointed. He reframed failure as not trying, rather than the outcome." — Sara Blakely
🎯 Key Takeaway: Reframe your relationship with failure. The entrepreneurs who win aren't the ones who avoid failure — they're the ones who normalise it. Make "attempts" your metric, not "successes."

3

Simon Sinek — Why Most Leaders Get It Wrong

The "Start With Why" author on leadership, trust, and infinite games

Simon Sinek went beyond his famous TED Talk to explain why so many entrepreneurs burn out despite building successful companies. His conversation with Bartlett explored the concept of the "infinite game" in business — why playing to win is fundamentally different from playing to keep playing, and why the latter produces companies that last for decades. This is essential listening for any founder thinking about company culture and long-term vision.

"Leadership is not about being in charge. It's about taking care of those in your charge." — Simon Sinek
🎯 Key Takeaway: Stop optimising for quarterly wins and start building an organisation people want to belong to. The companies that outlast their competitors invest in trust and culture before tactics and growth hacks.
→ Read the full episode summary
4

Chris Williamson — Discipline, Habits, and Modern Masculinity

The Modern Wisdom host on building unshakeable daily systems

Chris Williamson's multiple appearances on Diary of a CEO have produced some of the most practical content on the podcast. His episodes cover the psychology of habit formation, why discipline matters more than motivation, and how to structure your days for peak output. For entrepreneurs who struggle with consistency — which is almost all of them — this episode is a wake-up call delivered with scientific precision.

"You don't rise to the level of your goals. You fall to the level of your systems." — Chris Williamson (citing James Clear)
🎯 Key Takeaway: Design your environment and daily systems before relying on willpower. The most productive entrepreneurs don't have more motivation — they have better defaults and fewer decisions to make each day.
→ Read the full episode summary
5

Daniel Priestley — The Entrepreneur's Roadmap

A step-by-step framework for going from idea to scalable business

Daniel Priestley is a serial entrepreneur who has built multiple seven-figure businesses and helped thousands of founders through his company Dent Global. His Diary of a CEO episode is one of the most tactical on the entire podcast — he walks through the exact stages of entrepreneurship, common mistakes at each stage, and why most people stay stuck at "overworked operator" instead of building a genuine enterprise. If you feel like you're working harder than ever but the business isn't growing, this episode will tell you exactly why.

"Most people don't have a business. They have a job they created for themselves — and it's the worst job in the world because the boss is an idiot." — Daniel Priestley
🎯 Key Takeaway: There's a difference between being self-employed and being an entrepreneur. The transition happens when you stop selling your time and start building assets, systems, and teams that generate value without your daily presence.

6

Gary Vaynerchuk — Patience, Content, and Building Empires

The CEO of VaynerMedia on long-term thinking and attention arbitrage

Gary Vee's appearance on Diary of a CEO was different from his usual high-energy content. Steven Bartlett drew out a more reflective side, discussing how patience is actually the entrepreneur's greatest competitive advantage, why most founders quit three feet from gold, and how the attention economy will reshape every industry over the next decade. If you've dismissed Gary Vee as "just a motivational guy," this episode will change your mind.

"The reason I'm so patient is because I genuinely believe I'm going to do this for another 50 years. When you think in decades, you stop rushing quarters." — Gary Vaynerchuk
🎯 Key Takeaway: Extend your time horizon. Entrepreneurs who think in decades make fundamentally different (and better) decisions than those optimising for the next quarter. Patience compounded over years is the most underrated business strategy.

7

Dr. Andrew Huberman — The Neuroscience of Peak Performance

Stanford neuroscientist on optimising your brain for entrepreneurship

Dr. Andrew Huberman's episode isn't a typical "business" episode, but it might be the most important one on this list. He breaks down how sleep, light exposure, dopamine management, and stress protocols directly impact your ability to think clearly, make decisions, and sustain energy across 12-hour workdays. Every tactical business strategy fails if the operator's brain isn't functioning properly — and Huberman gives you the owner's manual.

"The single most important thing you can do for your brain, your body, and your business is to get morning sunlight within the first hour of waking. It sets every biological clock in your body." — Dr. Andrew Huberman
🎯 Key Takeaway: Your body is the hardware that runs your business. Optimise sleep, manage dopamine (reduce phone scrolling), and use morning sunlight to set your circadian rhythm. These aren't "wellness tips" — they're performance upgrades with measurable ROI.
→ Read the full episode summary
8

Shaan Puri — How to Find Business Ideas That Print Money

The My First Million host on idea generation and rapid execution

Shaan Puri's episode is a goldmine for aspiring entrepreneurs who are stuck in "idea paralysis." He shared his framework for evaluating business ideas, explained why most people overthink their first business, and revealed the exact criteria he uses to decide whether an opportunity is worth pursuing. His approach is refreshingly simple: look for businesses that are boring, proven, and underserved by technology — then execute faster than everyone else.

"The best businesses are the ones your friends think are boring. Exciting ideas have too much competition. Boring ideas have too much profit." — Shaan Puri
🎯 Key Takeaway: Stop looking for revolutionary ideas. The best entrepreneurial opportunities are existing businesses with bad customer experiences and outdated technology. Find a proven model, modernise it, and dominate a niche before expanding.
→ Read the full episode summary
9

Trinny Woodall — Building a Beauty Empire After 50

How Trinny London became a £250M brand through community-first strategy

Trinny Woodall's episode is a masterclass in building a direct-to-consumer brand in a crowded market. She explained how she used social media authenticity — going live without makeup, sharing her real struggles — to build a community that became her distribution channel. Her approach to product development, where customers co-create with the brand, challenges everything traditional business schools teach about market research. For entrepreneurs in e-commerce or consumer brands, this is essential listening.

"I didn't launch a beauty brand. I built a community of women who felt seen — and then I made products for them. The brand was the by-product of the relationship." — Trinny Woodall
🎯 Key Takeaway: Build the audience before the product. Community-first businesses have lower acquisition costs, higher retention, and a built-in feedback loop. If your customers feel ownership over the brand, they'll sell it for you.
→ Read the full episode summary
10

Steven Bartlett (Solo) — The 33 Laws of Business and Life

The host shares his own hard-won entrepreneurial principles

In this rare solo episode, Steven Bartlett distils everything he's learned from building Social Chain into a publicly traded company and interviewing hundreds of the world's most successful people. The 33 laws cover everything from hiring and firing to managing your psychology as a founder. It's raw, personal, and packed with the kind of specific advice that only someone who's been in the trenches can give. Many listeners consider this the single best episode of Diary of a CEO.

"You are the CEO of your life. Every day you're either building the company you want to live in, or you're accepting the default settings someone else chose for you." — Steven Bartlett
🎯 Key Takeaway: Entrepreneurship is a personal development journey disguised as a business one. The business will only grow as fast as you do. Invest in self-awareness, emotional intelligence, and the ability to have hard conversations — these are the real competitive advantages.

Why Diary of a CEO Is the Best Podcast for Entrepreneurs

What makes Diary of a CEO different from other business podcasts is Steven Bartlett's interviewing style. He doesn't ask surface-level questions or let guests recite their usual talking points. He pushes for specifics, shares his own vulnerabilities, and creates a space where guests reveal insights they haven't shared elsewhere.
