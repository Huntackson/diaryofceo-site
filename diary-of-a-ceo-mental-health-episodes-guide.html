Anxiety & Stress Episodes

Anxiety is the most common mental health topic on DOAC, and for good reason — it's the most common mental health condition in the world. These episodes offer practical, science-backed approaches to understanding and managing anxiety.

Dr. Julie Smith — "Why You Feel Anxious and How to Fix It"

Dr. Julie Smith — clinical psychologist and author of Why Has Nobody Told Me This Before? — delivered one of the most practically useful DOAC episodes ever recorded. She explained that anxiety is not a character flaw but a survival mechanism that's misfiring in a modern environment it wasn't designed for.

Her key tools: the "emotional first aid kit" (a pre-prepared set of coping strategies for anxious moments), cognitive defusion (learning to observe anxious thoughts without believing them), and the importance of understanding your anxiety window — the zone between "too comfortable" and "overwhelmed" where growth happens.

Smith also made a point that resonated with millions: "You don't need to eliminate anxiety. You need to change your relationship with it. Anxiety is a messenger, not an enemy."

Anxiety CBT Tools Practical

Best for: Anyone struggling with daily anxiety, overthinking, or panic

Andrew Huberman — "The Science of Fear and How to Control It"

Stanford neuroscientist Andrew Huberman brought the biology of anxiety to DOAC. He explained the sympathetic nervous system's fight-or-flight response in accessible language and, crucially, gave Bartlett specific tools to hack it.

His most impactful technique: physiological sighing — a double inhale through the nose followed by a long exhale through the mouth. This activates the parasympathetic nervous system within seconds, literally slowing your heart rate and reducing cortisol. Huberman said it's the fastest, most evidence-backed way to reduce acute anxiety without medication.

He also covered how morning sunlight resets your cortisol rhythm (reducing baseline anxiety), why cold exposure builds stress resilience, and how the visual system connects to anxiety states — widening your gaze literally calms your nervous system.

Neuroscience Anxiety Breathing Stress

Best for: People who want to understand the science behind anxiety and need immediate calming tools

Dr. Rangan Chatterjee — "The Stress Solution"

Dr. Chatterjee took a holistic approach to anxiety, arguing that most chronic stress isn't caused by one big thing but by the accumulation of micro-stresses: poor sleep, processed food, sedentary behaviour, social media, and shallow breathing. His "four pillar" approach (sleep, food, movement, relaxation) provides a framework for reducing baseline stress levels so that anxiety episodes become less frequent and less intense.

His most memorable advice to Bartlett: "You can't meditate your way out of a lifestyle that's designed to make you anxious."

Stress Holistic Health Lifestyle

Best for: People whose anxiety feels connected to lifestyle and overwhelm

Depression & Low Mood Episodes

DOAC's depression-focused episodes are some of the most powerful content on the internet — raw, honest conversations that have helped millions feel less alone.

Dr. Julie Smith — "The Hidden Signs of Depression"

In her second DOAC appearance, Dr. Smith focused specifically on depression. She explained that depression often doesn't look like sadness — it can manifest as numbness, irritability, physical pain, loss of interest, or simply feeling "flat." Many people don't recognise they're depressed because they're waiting to feel sad, when the reality is they feel nothing.

Smith's framework for managing depression: behavioural activation (doing things even when you don't feel like it, because mood follows action, not the other way around), social connection (even when every fibre of your being wants to isolate), and the "STOPP" technique — Stop, Take a breath, Observe your thoughts, Pull back from the thought, Practice what works.

Depression Practical Tools Recognition

Best for: Anyone experiencing low mood, numbness, or loss of motivation

Johann Hari — "Lost Connections: The Real Causes of Depression"

Journalist Johann Hari challenged the chemical imbalance theory of depression on DOAC. Drawing from his book Lost Connections, he argued that depression is primarily caused by disconnection — from meaningful work, from other people, from nature, from a hopeful future, and from childhood trauma. While he acknowledged that medication helps some people, he told Bartlett that treating depression purely as a brain chemistry problem ignores the social and environmental causes.

His most provocative point: "If you're depressed because you're lonely, isolated, doing meaningless work, and disconnected from nature, that's not a malfunction. That's your brain correctly identifying that something in your life needs to change."

Depression Social Connection Root Causes

Best for: People questioning whether their depression has deeper life-circumstance causes

Trauma & Healing Episodes

Some of DOAC's most profound episodes deal with trauma — its origins, its effects on the body and mind, and the paths to healing. These conversations have been life-changing for many listeners.

Dr. Gabor Maté — "Trauma Is Not What Happens to You"

Dr. Gabor Maté's DOAC episode is widely considered one of the most important podcast episodes ever recorded. The renowned physician and trauma expert fundamentally reframed how millions of people understand trauma.

His central thesis: trauma is not what happens to you — it's what happens inside you as a result of what happens to you. Specifically, trauma is the disconnection from your authentic self that occurs when, as a child, you learn that expressing your true feelings threatens your attachment to your caregivers.

Maté explained that a child who learns "I must suppress my anger to keep mummy's love" doesn't just suppress anger in childhood — they build an entire personality structure around suppression. Decades later, this manifests as anxiety, depression, autoimmune disease, addiction, or chronic people-pleasing.

The path to healing, Maté told Bartlett, is reconnecting with those suppressed emotions. This often requires therapy, but it starts with simply asking: "What did I have to give up about myself in order to survive my childhood?"

Bartlett was visibly moved during this episode, and later called it the most impactful conversation he'd ever had.

Trauma Childhood Healing Addiction

Best for: Anyone who suspects their current struggles are rooted in childhood experiences

Dr. Paul Conti — "Understanding and Healing Trauma"

Psychiatrist Dr. Paul Conti provided a clinical framework for understanding trauma that complements Maté's more philosophical approach. Conti explained the difference between "big T" trauma (abuse, accidents, war) and "little t" trauma (chronic emotional neglect, bullying, family dysfunction) — and made the crucial point that "little t" trauma can be just as damaging because it's insidious and harder to identify.

His practical advice: trauma healing isn't about reliving painful experiences. It's about processing the emotions those experiences created but that were never safely expressed. This can happen through therapy (particularly EMDR, somatic experiencing, and psychodynamic therapy), through relationships, and through creative expression.

Trauma Psychiatry Healing Methods

Best for: People seeking clinical understanding of trauma and evidence-based healing approaches

Self-Worth & Confidence Episodes

Issues of self-worth run beneath almost every mental health struggle. These episodes tackle the core question: "Am I enough?"

Jay Shetty — "How to Find Your Purpose and Build Self-Worth"

Former monk turned motivational speaker Jay Shetty shared his framework for building authentic self-worth on DOAC. His central argument: most people's self-worth is conditional — based on achievements, appearance, wealth, or others' approval. True self-worth, he told Bartlett, is unconditional — rooted in the simple fact that you exist and have inherent value.

Shetty's practical exercises: daily journaling focused on values (not goals), a "social media audit" to unfollow accounts that trigger comparison, and what he calls "service practice" — regularly doing something for others without recognition, which builds self-worth that isn't dependent on external validation.

Self-Worth Purpose Mindfulness

Best for: People struggling with self-doubt, comparison, or feeling "not enough"

Derren Brown — "The Happiness Illusion"

Mentalist and philosopher Derren Brown offered perhaps the most intellectually stimulating mental health conversation on DOAC. He argued that the modern pursuit of happiness is itself a cause of unhappiness. Drawing from Stoic philosophy, Brown told Bartlett that chasing positive emotions and avoiding negative ones creates a fragile psychology. True resilience comes from accepting the full spectrum of human experience.

His most memorable point: "The self-help industry sells you the idea that you should always be improving, always growing, always optimising. But sometimes the most radical act of self-care is to accept that you're fine as you are — right now, in this imperfect moment."

Happiness Stoicism Self-Acceptance

Best for: Overthinkers and self-improvement addicts who feel exhausted by constant striving

Brené Brown — "Vulnerability Is Your Greatest Strength"

Shame researcher Brené Brown told Bartlett that vulnerability — the willingness to be seen in your imperfection — is not weakness but the birthplace of connection, creativity, and courage. She explained that shame (the belief "I am bad") drives people to hide, numb, and perform, while guilt (the belief "I did something bad") actually motivates positive change.

Her advice: practise "shame resilience" by sharing your struggles with people who have earned the right to hear them. Not everyone deserves your vulnerability — but the right people will meet it with empathy, and that connection is the antidote to shame.

Vulnerability Shame Confidence

Best for: People who struggle with perfectionism, people-pleasing, or fear of judgement

Relationships & Attachment Episodes

Mental health doesn't exist in a vacuum — our relationships are often both the cause of and the cure for psychological distress. These DOAC episodes explore the intersection of relationships and mental health.

Alain de Botton — "Why We Choose the Wrong Partners"

Philosopher Alain de Botton offered a psychoanalytic perspective on why people repeatedly choose partners who make them unhappy. His thesis: we don't fall in love with people who are good for us — we fall in love with people who feel familiar. If your childhood was marked by emotional unavailability, you'll be unconsciously drawn to emotionally unavailable partners because that dynamic feels like "home."

De Botton's solution isn't to find a "perfect" partner but to become aware of your attachment patterns and consciously choose differently — even when "differently" feels strange or boring at first.

Relationships Attachment Psychology

Best for: People stuck in unhealthy relationship patterns

Addiction & Habits Episodes

Dr. Gabor Maté — "The Root of All Addiction"

In the same landmark episode, Maté explained that addiction is never about the substance or behaviour — it's about pain. "The question is never 'why the addiction?' but always 'why the pain?'" he told Bartlett. Addiction is an attempt to solve a problem — to soothe emotional pain that the person has no other way to manage.

This reframing — from addiction as moral failure to addiction as pain management — has helped millions of DOAC listeners develop compassion for themselves and others struggling with addictive behaviours, whether substances, social media, food, or work.

Addiction Trauma Compassion

Best for: Anyone struggling with addictive patterns or loving someone who does

Anna Lembke — "Dopamine Nation: The Hidden Cost of Pleasure"

Stanford psychiatrist Anna Lembke explained the neuroscience of addiction in terrifyingly clear terms. The brain's pleasure-pain balance means that every hit of pleasure (scrolling, sugar, porn, substances) is followed by an equal and opposite pain response. Over time, the brain recalibrates, requiring more stimulation for less pleasure — the classic addiction cycle.

Her advice: practice "dopamine fasting" — deliberate periods of abstaining from high-dopamine behaviours to reset the brain's baseline. She told Bartlett that the first 2-4 weeks are the hardest, but after that, people report finding genuine pleasure in simple activities again.

Dopamine Addiction Neuroscience

Best for: People who feel addicted to their phone, social media, or any compulsive behaviour

Sleep & Mental Health Episodes

Matthew Walker — "Sleep Is Your Superpower"

Matthew Walker's episode connected sleep directly to mental health in ways that are hard to ignore. He told Bartlett that sleep deprivation increases activity in the amygdala (the brain's fear centre) by 60%, while simultaneously reducing prefrontal cortex activity (your rational brain). The result: sleep-deprived people are more anxious, more reactive, more emotionally volatile, and less capable of rational thought.

Walker's chilling statement: "There is no major psychiatric disorder in which sleep is normal." Depression, anxiety, PTSD, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia — all are characterised by disrupted sleep. Improving sleep doesn't cure these conditions, but it's almost impossible to heal from any of them without addressing sleep first.

Sleep Mental Health Neuroscience

Best for: Anyone whose mental health struggles coincide with poor sleep

Steven Bartlett's Own Mental Health Journey

What sets DOAC apart from other interview podcasts is Steven Bartlett's willingness to be as vulnerable as his guests. Across multiple episodes and solo reflections, Bartlett has opened up about his own mental health struggles with remarkable honesty.

Loneliness and Isolation

Bartlett has spoken candidly about the loneliness of success. Building Social Chain consumed his twenties — he was surrounded by employees and investors but genuinely close to very few people. "I had a hundred people in my phone I could call about business," he told one guest. "But almost no one I could call at 2 AM when I was falling apart."

Imposter Syndrome

Despite his achievements, Bartlett has repeatedly discussed feeling like a fraud. Becoming the youngest Dragon on Dragons' Den amplified this: "Every week I sat there thinking, 'They're going to figure out I don't belong here.'" His advice for dealing with imposter syndrome: accept it as a permanent companion that shows up whenever you're doing something meaningful, rather than treating it as a problem to solve.

Childhood and Family

Bartlett's discussions about his childhood — growing up on a council estate, his parents' separation, the financial instability — reveal the origin of his relentless drive. After his conversation with Gabor Maté, Bartlett publicly reflected on how his childhood shaped patterns he's spent years trying to understand: the workaholism, the need for control, the difficulty trusting others with responsibility.

Therapy and Growth

Bartlett has been open about attending therapy and crediting it with significant personal growth. He's normalised therapy for his largely young, male audience — a demographic that traditionally resists mental health support. "Going to therapy doesn't mean you're broken," Bartlett has said. "It means you're brave enough to look at the parts of yourself most people spend their whole lives running from."

"I used to think success would fix everything. Now I know that success just gives you the resources to confront everything you've been avoiding." — Steven Bartlett

10 Key Lessons from Every DOAC Mental Health Episode

After analysing every mental health episode on the Diary of a CEO, these ten themes emerge consistently:

  1. Your childhood matters more than you think. Almost every mental health expert on DOAC traces adult struggles back to childhood experiences — not necessarily abuse, but patterns of attachment, emotional expression, and belief formation.
  2. Anxiety and depression are signals, not diseases. They indicate that something in your life, relationships, or thought patterns needs attention.
  3. Sleep is non-negotiable. Every single neuroscientist and doctor on DOAC puts sleep at the foundation of mental health.
  4. Connection heals. Loneliness, isolation, and disconnection are at the root of most mental health crises. Rebuilding connection — even imperfectly — is therapeutic.
  5. Feelings need to be felt. Suppressing, numbing, or avoiding emotions doesn't make them go away — it makes them stronger and more destructive.
  6. Vulnerability is strength. Brené Brown, Bartlett, and others consistently demonstrate that admitting struggle builds connection and resilience.
  7. Therapy works. Multiple guests and Bartlett himself advocate for professional help as a powerful tool, not a last resort.
  8. Your body and mind are one system. Exercise, nutrition, sleep, and sunlight have profound effects on psychological wellbeing.
  9. Self-compassion beats self-criticism. Beating yourself up doesn't motivate change — it fuels shame cycles. Treat yourself with the kindness you'd offer a friend.
  10. Recovery isn't linear. Every expert on DOAC acknowledges that mental health is a journey with setbacks. The goal isn't perfection — it's a gradually upward trajectory.

Where Should You Start?

With 40+ mental health episodes to choose from, here are our recommendations based on what you're going through:

  • If you're dealing with anxiety: Start with Dr. Julie Smith's first episode, then watch Huberman for the neuroscience tools.
  • If you're feeling depressed or numb: Start with Johann Hari's episode for understanding, then Dr. Julie Smith's depression episode for practical tools.
  • If you suspect childhood issues are affecting you: Watch Dr. Gabor Maté's episode. It will be uncomfortable but potentially transformative.
  • If you're struggling with self-worth: Start with Jay Shetty, then Brené Brown, then Derren Brown for three complementary perspectives.
  • If you're in a toxic relationship pattern: Watch Alain de Botton's episode, then explore our relationship advice guide.
  • If you can't sleep: Matthew Walker's episode is essential. Also read our Walker sleep advice summary.
  • If you just want to feel better overall: Start with Dr. Rangan Chatterjee for a holistic lifestyle approach.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the best Diary of a CEO episodes about mental health?

The best DOAC mental health episodes feature Dr. Julie Smith on anxiety and depression, Dr. Gabor Maté on trauma and addiction, Jay Shetty on purpose and self-worth, Matthew Walker on sleep, Dr. Paul Conti on trauma processing, Derren Brown on happiness, and Brené Brown on vulnerability. Steven Bartlett's own vulnerable discussions are also essential listening.

Does Steven Bartlett talk about his own mental health on DOAC?

Yes, Steven Bartlett has been remarkably open about his mental health. He's discussed loneliness, imposter syndrome, the psychological cost of success, childhood trauma, and the pressure of running multiple businesses. His vulnerability has become a defining feature of the podcast and has normalised mental health conversations for his young male audience.

Which DOAC episode should I watch if I'm struggling with anxiety?

Start with Dr. Julie Smith's episodes for practical, evidence-based tools. Then watch Andrew Huberman's episode for neuroscience-backed techniques like physiological sighing. Dr. Gabor Maté's episode is essential if you suspect your anxiety has roots in childhood experiences.

What does Dr. Gabor Maté say about trauma on Diary of a CEO?

Dr. Maté told Steven Bartlett that trauma isn't what happens to you — it's what happens inside you as a result. Most addiction, anxiety, and depression stems from unprocessed childhood trauma, and healing requires reconnecting with the emotions we learned to suppress as children.

Are DOAC mental health episodes a substitute for therapy?

No. While DOAC episodes provide valuable psychoeducation from world-class experts, they are not a substitute for professional therapy or medical treatment. They can complement therapy and normalise mental health conversations, but anyone struggling should seek professional help.

How many mental health episodes does Diary of a CEO have?

As of 2026, DOAC has over 40 episodes significantly focused on mental health, covering anxiety, depression, trauma, addiction, self-worth, loneliness, grief, burnout, and emotional regulation. Many other episodes touch on mental health themes even when the primary topic is business or health.
