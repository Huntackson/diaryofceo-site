Head-to-Head Comparisons

Diary of a CEO vs The Joe Rogan Experience VS

Joe Rogan's podcast is the OG long-form conversation show, and its influence on the medium cannot be overstated. Rogan covers everything from MMA to politics to alien conspiracies, and his massive audience proves the appeal of his wide-net approach.

But that's exactly where DOAC differentiates. While Rogan is a generalist, Steven Bartlett is a specialist — in the intersection of business, psychology, and personal growth. When you tune into Diary of a CEO, you know what you're getting: a deep conversation that will make you think differently about success, relationships, or human behavior.

The interviewing style is also fundamentally different. Rogan often lets conversations meander (which can be brilliant or frustrating). Steven is more intentional — he clearly prepares extensively, guides the conversation through key themes, and isn't afraid to interrupt with pointed follow-up questions. The result is a tighter, more focused listening experience.

Rogan's 3+ hour episodes are a commitment. DOAC's 1.5-hour format hits a sweet spot — long enough for depth, short enough for a commute or gym session.

Choose DOAC if: You want focused, business-relevant conversations with emotional depth. Choose JRE if: You want wide-ranging, unpredictable conversations across every topic imaginable.

Diary of a CEO vs Lex Fridman Podcast VS

Lex Fridman is one of the most thoughtful interviewers alive. His conversations with scientists, philosophers, and tech leaders are often profound and deeply intellectual. If you want to understand the nature of consciousness or the future of AI, Lex is your man.

The difference is accessibility. Lex's interviews are cerebral — sometimes to the point of being inaccessible for casual listeners. Steven Bartlett has a gift for making complex ideas feel intuitive. He translates academic research and high-level business strategy into language and stories that anyone can relate to.

There's also an emotional dimension that DOAC has that Lex's show often doesn't. Steven creates moments of genuine vulnerability — guests crying, sharing things they've never said publicly, confronting uncomfortable truths. Lex's conversations are more rational and measured. Both are valuable, but they serve different needs.

Guest overlap is minimal. Lex focuses heavily on scientists, researchers, and technologists. Steven brings on a broader mix: entrepreneurs, athletes, psychologists, doctors, entertainers, and occasionally people who are simply extraordinary humans with extraordinary stories.

Choose DOAC if: You want emotionally intelligent conversations that blend business and personal growth. Choose Lex if: You want deep intellectual dives into science, technology, and philosophy.

Diary of a CEO vs My First Million VS

My First Million with Shaan Puri and Sam Parr is one of the most entertaining business podcasts out there. Their chemistry is electric, and the show is packed with tactical business ideas, revenue breakdowns, and "steal this idea" segments that make you want to start a business immediately.

The key difference: MFM is about the tactics of making money. DOAC is about the psychology of building a meaningful life and business. Both are valuable, but they operate at different altitudes. MFM gives you specific business models and market opportunities. DOAC gives you the mindset, frameworks, and self-awareness to execute on any opportunity.

MFM is also a co-hosted show with banter and debate. DOAC is a one-on-one interview format. If you love the "two friends riffing on ideas" format, MFM is unmatched. If you prefer going deep with a world-class guest, DOAC is the better choice.

Choose DOAC if: You want deep guest conversations that change how you think. Choose MFM if: You want rapid-fire business ideas and tactical money-making frameworks.

Diary of a CEO vs How I Built This VS

Guy Raz's How I Built This is a beautifully produced show that tells the origin stories of iconic companies — Airbnb, Instagram, Patagonia, and hundreds more. It's narrative journalism at its best, and it's genuinely inspiring.

The fundamental difference is format. HIBT is a polished, edited, story-driven show. It's crafted for a clean listening experience. DOAC is raw and unedited — you get the pauses, the stumbles, the real-time thinking. That rawness creates authenticity that highly produced shows can't replicate.

HIBT also tends to focus on the story — the chronological journey from idea to success. DOAC goes deeper into the psychology: why did you make that decision? What were you feeling? What did that cost you personally? Steven's willingness to sit in uncomfortable silences and ask "but why?" repeatedly extracts insights that a narrative format often glosses over.

HIBT episodes are shorter (around 50 minutes), which makes them more digestible but less deep. DOAC's longer format allows conversations to reach places that shorter shows simply can't access.

Choose DOAC if: You want raw, deep, psychologically rich conversations. Choose HIBT if: You want beautifully told origin stories of famous companies.

Diary of a CEO vs The Tim Ferriss Show VS

Tim Ferriss is a pioneer of the interview podcast format and his show has produced countless iconic episodes. Tim's approach is methodical — he famously asks structured questions about morning routines, recommended books, and "what would you put on a billboard?"

Steven's approach is more intuitive and emotionally driven. While Tim extracts tactical frameworks and life hacks, Steven extracts emotional truth and psychological insight. Tim's guests often sound like they're giving a masterclass. Steven's guests often sound like they're in therapy — in the best possible way.

Tim has also slowed his publishing cadence significantly in recent years, while DOAC releases multiple episodes per week. For listeners who want a regular fix of high-quality long-form content, Steven's consistency is a major advantage.

Choose DOAC if: You want frequent, emotionally rich conversations. Choose Ferriss if: You want methodical, framework-heavy interviews with a performance optimization angle.

The 5 Things That Make Diary of a CEO Unique

After comparing DOAC to every major business podcast, five differentiators emerge consistently:

1. Steven Bartlett Is Actually an Entrepreneur

This matters more than people realize. Many podcast hosts are journalists, commentators, or media personalities. Steven built a company from nothing to a public listing. When he talks to entrepreneurs, there's a mutual understanding that changes the texture of the conversation. Guests share things with Steven they wouldn't share with an interviewer who hasn't lived it.

2. The Emotional Depth Is Unmatched

DOAC regularly produces moments of raw emotion that other business podcasts never reach. Guests cry. They share trauma, regret, fear, and joy in ways that feel genuinely unprecedented for the genre. This isn't manipulation — it's the result of Steven creating a safe, judgment-free space and asking questions that cut beneath the professional veneer.

3. The Guest Range Is Extraordinary

In a single month, DOAC might feature a neuroscientist, a billionaire investor, an Olympic athlete, a relationship therapist, a former cult member, and a tech CEO. This range keeps the show unpredictable and ensures that listeners are constantly exposed to perspectives outside their usual bubble.

4. Production Quality Without Losing Authenticity

The DOAC set is iconic — the black backdrop, the two chairs, the intimate lighting. The production quality is world-class. But the conversations remain unedited and raw. It's the rare combination of looking polished while feeling real. Other shows tend to sacrifice one for the other.

5. The "Laws of Life" Framework

Steven's signature closing ritual — asking each guest to add to "The Diary of a CEO" book and share their "law of life" — creates a through-line that connects every episode. It's a simple device, but it gives listeners a single, memorable takeaway from each conversation. Over hundreds of episodes, this collection of "laws" has become a crowd-sourced philosophy of success.
