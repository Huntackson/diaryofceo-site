What Is Imposter Syndrome (And Why Do High Achievers Get It Worst)?

Imposter syndrome isn't a diagnosis — it's a pattern of thinking where you believe your success is undeserved, your competence is a performance, and it's only a matter of time before everyone discovers you're a fraud. First described by psychologists Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes in 1978, it was originally observed in high-achieving women — but research has since shown it affects everyone regardless of gender, age, or industry.

Here's the cruel irony: the more competent you are, the more likely you are to experience it. This is because high achievers set higher standards, compare themselves to more accomplished peers, and are acutely aware of everything they don't know. Meanwhile, the truly incompetent often have zero self-doubt — a phenomenon psychologists call the Dunning-Kruger effect.

Multiple guests on Diary of a CEO have tackled this topic from different angles — vulnerability, neuroscience, mindset, and identity. What follows is the best of what they've shared.

Brené Brown: Vulnerability Is the Antidote to Shame

Brené Brown's Diary of a CEO episode is one of the most emotionally powerful conversations the show has ever produced. The University of Houston research professor has spent over two decades studying shame, vulnerability, and courage — and her insights on imposter syndrome cut deeper than any self-help platitude.

Brown's central thesis: imposter syndrome is a shame response. It's not really about competence — it's about worthiness. The internal voice doesn't say "you lack the skills for this job." It says "you are not enough." That distinction matters, because you can acquire skills, but if you believe you're fundamentally inadequate, no amount of achievement will quiet the voice.

Key Takeaways from Brené Brown

  • Shame thrives in secrecy: The moment you say "I feel like a fraud" out loud to someone you trust, shame loses its power. Brown found in her research that shame cannot survive being spoken — it requires silence and hiding to grow.
  • Vulnerability is not weakness — it's courage: Showing up when you can't control the outcome is the definition of bravery. Every time you raise your hand, pitch an idea, or start something new despite feeling unqualified, you're being courageous — not foolish.
  • The "arena" metaphor: Brown quotes Theodore Roosevelt's "Man in the Arena" speech. If you're in the arena — trying, building, risking — you will get knocked down. The critics sitting in the cheap seats, who never risk anything, don't get to have an opinion on your worthiness.
  • Develop "shame resilience": This isn't about eliminating shame (that's impossible). It's about recognizing shame when it shows up, reaching out to someone you trust, and telling your story. Brown calls this practice "shame resilience," and it's a skill you build over time.
💡 Best for: Anyone whose imposter syndrome is rooted in a deep belief that they're not "enough" — not smart enough, experienced enough, or deserving enough. Brown gets to the emotional core of the issue.

Steven Bartlett: "I Still Feel Like a Fraud"

Steven Bartlett has built multiple businesses, become the youngest Dragon on Dragons' Den, and hosts one of the world's most popular podcasts. And yet, across numerous episodes, he has been remarkably transparent about one thing: he still feels like an imposter.

In his conversations with guests and in his solo reflections, Steven has described the persistent voice that tells him he got lucky, that he doesn't deserve his success, and that eventually people will see through him. What makes his honesty so powerful is the context — here's a man who has every external marker of success, and he's still fighting the same battle as someone in their first week at a new job.

Steven's Insights on Imposter Syndrome

  • "Imposter syndrome is the tax on ambition": Steven reframes the feeling not as a bug, but as a signal that you're pushing beyond your comfort zone. If you never feel like a fraud, you're probably not growing.
  • The "evidence file" technique: Steven keeps a running list of his achievements, positive feedback, and moments of genuine competence. When imposter syndrome strikes, he reviews the evidence — not to stroke his ego, but to counterbalance the distorted thinking with facts.
  • Surround yourself with people who are honest: Not people who flatter you, and not people who tear you down — people who tell you the truth. Steven's inner circle includes advisors who will say "you're wrong" and "you're doing better than you think" with equal honesty.
  • Action is the cure: Steven has said repeatedly that confidence doesn't come before action — it comes from action. You don't wait until you feel ready. You start, fail, learn, and the confidence builds on the back of experience.

Mel Robbins: The 5-Second Rule for Self-Doubt

Mel Robbins is one of the most-requested guests in DOAC history, and her approach to imposter syndrome is refreshingly practical. Where Brené Brown goes deep on the emotional roots, Robbins gives you a tool you can use in the next 60 seconds.

Robbins' insight: imposter syndrome is your brain's protection mechanism firing at the wrong time. Your amygdala (the brain's fear center) can't tell the difference between "a tiger is chasing you" and "you're about to give a presentation to the board." Both trigger the same fight-or-flight response. The result? Your brain screams "you're not safe" — which your conscious mind interprets as "you don't belong here."

Key Takeaways from Mel Robbins

  • The 5-4-3-2-1 technique: When imposter syndrome hits — before a meeting, a pitch, a conversation — count backwards from 5 and then physically move. The countdown interrupts the brain's habit loop and activates the prefrontal cortex, which handles rational decision-making instead of fear.
  • "Anxiety is excitement without breathing": Robbins explained that the physical sensations of anxiety and excitement are neurologically identical — racing heart, sweaty palms, butterflies. The only difference is the story you tell yourself. Reframe "I'm nervous" as "I'm excited" and your brain literally changes its response.
  • Self-worth comes from kept promises: Every time you tell yourself you'll do something and follow through, you deposit into your self-trust account. Every broken promise is a withdrawal. Imposter syndrome is partly the result of a depleted self-trust account — too many times telling yourself "I'll start tomorrow" and not starting.
  • The "Let Them" theory applied to critics: When someone questions your qualifications or doubts your ability — let them. You cannot control what others think. You can only control whether you show up and do the work.
💡 Best for: Anyone who needs an immediate, actionable technique to push through imposter syndrome in real-time. Robbins doesn't ask you to journal for a month — she gives you a tool that works in 5 seconds.

Jordan Peterson: Compare Yourself to Who You Were Yesterday

Jordan Peterson's appearance on Diary of a CEO produced one of the most-quoted lines in the show's history: "Compare yourself to who you were yesterday, not to who someone else is today." This single idea has become a mantra for millions of people struggling with self-doubt.

Peterson's argument is that imposter syndrome is often fueled by social comparison — looking at someone five years ahead of you and concluding you're behind. But you're not running their race. You're running yours. The only meaningful benchmark is whether you're better today than you were yesterday, even by 1%.

Key Takeaways from Jordan Peterson

  • Take responsibility for your own development: Peterson argues that the most powerful antidote to feeling like a fraud is taking ownership of your growth. Set small, achievable goals every day. Hit them. Stack evidence that you're improving.
  • "Set your house in perfect order before you criticize the world": Instead of worrying about whether you deserve your seat at the table, focus on the things within your control — your preparation, your effort, your integrity. Master the basics and confidence follows.
  • Meaning comes from shouldering responsibility: Peterson's deeper point is that imposter syndrome often reflects a lack of meaning, not a lack of competence. When you're clear about why you're doing something — when the work serves a purpose bigger than your ego — the question of whether you "deserve" to be there becomes irrelevant.
  • Articulate your thoughts: Peterson emphasizes the power of clear thinking and speaking. If you can articulate what you know, you realize you know more than you thought. Many people with imposter syndrome have genuine expertise but can't access it under pressure because they've never practiced expressing it clearly.

Jay Shetty: You're Not the Voice in Your Head

Jay Shetty, the former monk turned motivational powerhouse, brought a spiritual dimension to the imposter syndrome conversation on DOAC. His insight is deceptively simple but profound: you are not the voice in your head. You are the one listening to it.

Shetty explains that imposter syndrome is essentially your inner critic — a voice that developed in childhood to protect you from risk, rejection, and failure. The problem is that this voice doesn't update itself. It still thinks you're the kid who got laughed at in class, even though you're now a competent adult. The solution isn't to silence the voice — it's to stop believing everything it says.

Key Takeaways from Jay Shetty

  • Observe your thoughts without believing them: Shetty uses meditation to create distance between himself and his inner critic. When the thought "you're a fraud" arrives, he doesn't fight it or agree with it — he simply notices it, like watching a cloud pass. This is called "cognitive defusion" in psychology.
  • Your identity is not your achievements: One reason imposter syndrome is so painful is that we tie our self-worth to our accomplishments. Shetty argues that you are not your job title, your salary, or your followers. When your identity is anchored to something deeper — values, service, growth — external success or failure doesn't threaten your core.
  • Service dissolves self-doubt: Shetty's most practical advice — when you're stuck in your head worrying about whether you're good enough, shift your focus to how you can serve others. Self-doubt is inherently self-focused. Service forces you outward, and the inner critic has no ammunition when you're genuinely helping someone.

The 5-Step Anti-Imposter Framework from DOAC Guests

After analyzing dozens of Diary of a CEO episodes that touch on self-doubt, confidence, and imposter syndrome, we've distilled the collective wisdom into a single, actionable framework:

Step 1: Name It (Brené Brown)

The moment you feel like a fraud, say it out loud — to yourself or someone you trust. "I'm experiencing imposter syndrome right now." This immediately creates distance between you and the feeling. Shame cannot survive being named.

Step 2: Interrupt It (Mel Robbins)

Count 5-4-3-2-1 and physically move. Stand up, change position, walk. The countdown activates your prefrontal cortex and breaks the spiral of anxious thinking. Reframe: "I'm not nervous — I'm excited."

Step 3: Evidence-Check It (Steven Bartlett)

Open your evidence file. Review three concrete examples of your competence, positive feedback you've received, or problems you've solved. Imposter syndrome is a feeling — counter it with facts.

Step 4: Redirect It (Jordan Peterson)

Ask: "Am I better at this than I was six months ago?" If yes, you're on the right track. Stop comparing yourself to people who started before you. Your only competition is your past self.

Step 5: Serve Through It (Jay Shetty)

Shift your focus from "Am I good enough?" to "How can I help?" Self-doubt is self-focused. When you're genuinely trying to add value to someone else's life, the inner critic loses its stage.

The 5 Types of Imposter Syndrome (And Which DOAC Guest Matches Each)

Research by Dr. Valerie Young identifies five imposter types. Here's which DOAC advice works best for each:

  1. The Perfectionist — You set impossibly high standards and feel like a failure when you don't meet them. Watch: James Clear on systems over goals — perfection is the enemy of consistency.
  2. The Superwoman/Man — You work harder than everyone to "prove" you belong, risking burnout. Watch: Simon Sinek on the infinite game — there's no "winning" to prove yourself.
  3. The Natural Genius — You think competence should come easily, so struggle feels like proof of inadequacy. Watch: Angela Duckworth on grit — effort is not a sign of weakness.
  4. The Soloist — You think asking for help proves you're a fraud. Watch: Brené Brown on vulnerability — connection, not isolation, is the answer.
  5. The Expert — You'll never feel like you know enough to deserve your title. Watch: Naval Ravikant on specific knowledge — your unique perspective is your qualification.

Frequently Asked Questions

What did Brené Brown say about imposter syndrome on Diary of a CEO?

Brené Brown explained that imposter syndrome is rooted in shame — the fear of being exposed as not enough. She said vulnerability is not weakness but the birthplace of courage, and that the antidote is sharing your story with people who have earned the right to hear it. Her full episode is one of the best mental health episodes on the show.

Does Steven Bartlett have imposter syndrome?

Yes. Steven has openly discussed feeling like a fraud throughout his career, even after building Social Chain into a multi-million pound company and becoming the youngest Dragon on Dragons' Den. He's said that imposter syndrome never fully goes away — you just learn to act despite it and build an evidence file of your competence.

How do you stop feeling like a fraud at work?

According to DOAC guests: (1) Recognize that 70% of people experience imposter syndrome — it's normal, (2) use Mel Robbins' 5-4-3-2-1 technique to interrupt spiraling thoughts, (3) keep an evidence file of accomplishments, (4) compare yourself only to your past self (Jordan Peterson), and (5) shift focus from self-doubt to service (Jay Shetty).

What is the best Diary of a CEO episode about self-doubt?

The Brené Brown episode is the definitive DOAC conversation on shame and self-doubt. For practical tools, Mel Robbins' episode on the Let Them Theory and 5 Second Rule is excellent. For a mindset shift, Jordan Peterson's episode on personal responsibility resonates deeply.

Is imposter syndrome a mental health condition?

No — imposter syndrome is not a clinical diagnosis. It's a psychological pattern that most people experience to some degree. However, when it becomes persistent and debilitating, it can overlap with anxiety and depression. If imposter syndrome is significantly impacting your daily life, speaking with a mental health professional is recommended.
